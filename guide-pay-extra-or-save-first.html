Pay extra on your student loan, or save first?

A plain-English guide · 4 minute read

You have a bit of spare money each month. Should it go towards your loan, or into savings? The answer depends on a few simple things. Here's how to think it through.

Step 1: Do you have a small safety cushion?

If a surprise bill would force you to borrow more, build a small cushion first. Many people aim for at least one month of basic costs before paying extra on a loan. Without a cushion, an emergency can push you into higher-interest debt.

Step 2: Compare the two rates

Paying extra on a loan works like earning that loan's interest rate, with no risk. Saving earns your savings account's rate. Whichever rate is higher usually wins.

Your situationWhat it points to
Loan rate is well above your savings rateExtra payments tend to make sense
Loan rate is low and savings pay about the sameEither choice works, so do what feels safer
No cushion at allBuild a small one first

Some people use about 7% as a rough line for "high" loan interest. That's a rule of thumb, not a law.

What extra payments really do

Take a $10,000 loan at 6% a year, paying $150 a month. You'd be paid off in 6 years 10 months and about $2,194 in interest.

Add just $50 a month ($200 total) and you finish in 4 years 10 months and about $1,536 in interest. That's 2 years sooner and about $658 less interest.

Check these before paying extra

  • Prepayment fees. Some private loans charge them. Ask your lender.
  • Where the money goes. Tell your lender the extra should go to the balance, not to next month's payment.
  • Different loans, different rates. If you have several, extra money on the highest-rate loan saves the most.

A simple way to decide

  • No cushion? Save a small one first.
  • Cushion done and a high loan rate? Put extra towards the loan.
  • Low loan rate? Splitting the extra between both is a fair choice.
