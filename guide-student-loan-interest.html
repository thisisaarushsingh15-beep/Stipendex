How student loan interest really works

A plain-English guide · 4 minute read

Your loan balance can feel like it barely moves. That's not your imagination. Here's what's happening, in simple steps.

Interest is a monthly fee on what you still owe

Your lender charges a percentage of your balance every year. Split across the months, that's a small fee added each month. The bigger your balance, the bigger the fee.

Example: you owe $10,000 at 6% a year. That's 0.5% a month, so the first month's interest is $50.

Your payment pays interest first

Each payment covers the month's interest first. Only what's left over reduces your balance.

Pay $150 on that $10,000 loan: $50 covers interest and $100 lowers your balance to $9,900. Next month the interest is a little smaller, about $49.50, so slightly more of your payment goes to the balance. This is why early payments feel slow and later ones speed up.

Why your balance can grow while you pay

If your payment is smaller than the monthly interest, the extra interest gets added to what you owe. On the same loan, paying only $30 a month means $20 of interest is left over each month, so the balance goes up. Some low-payment repayment plans work like this, so check yours.

Interest can start before you graduate

Some student loans add interest while you're still studying. Others wait until after you leave. It depends on the type of loan and the country, so check with your lender. Interest that builds up early can be added to your balance later, which makes future interest bigger.

Why extra payments help so much

Every extra dollar goes straight to your balance. A lower balance means less interest next month, and that snowballs. Paying a bit extra early saves more than paying the same extra later.

What to do next

  • Find your loan's interest rate and current balance.
  • Check that your payment is higher than one month of interest.
  • Try adding a small extra amount and see how much time it saves.
